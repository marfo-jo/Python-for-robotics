## 1. The voltage divider

Two resistors in series between Vin and ground. With no load on the output, the same current flows through both:

$$I = \frac{V_{in}}{R_1 + R_2}$$

The output is the voltage across R2 (the bottom resistor):

$$V_{out} = I \cdot R_2 = V_{in} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

Two sanity checks that catch most mistakes: if R2 is much larger than R1, Vout is close to Vin. If R1 = R2, Vout is exactly half of Vin. **The bottom resistor is on top of the fraction.**

### Worked example 1: 5 V down to about 3.3 V

R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 20 kΩ, Vin = 5 V:

$$V_{out} = 5 \cdot \frac{20}{10 + 20} = 3.33 \text{ V}$$

The same ratio with 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ gives the same 3.33 V. The ratio sets the voltage; the **magnitude** sets the current (1.67 mA for 1k + 2k, 0.17 mA for 10k + 20k) and how much the output sags under load (section 2).

### Worked example 2: reading a 12.6 V battery with a 5 V ADC

A charged 3-cell lithium pack is 12.6 V. The Uno's ADC reads 0 to 5 V. Choose R1 = 20 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ:

$$V_{out} = 12.6 \cdot \frac{10}{20 + 10} = 4.2 \text{ V}$$

That's under 5 V with some margin. The 10-bit ADC reading is about

$$\frac{4.2}{5} \cdot 1023 \approx 859$$

(860 if you use the 1024 convention; the difference is one count). In firmware you reverse it: battery volts = reading × (5 / 1023) × 3. The divider draws 12.6 V / 30 kΩ = 0.42 mA all the time, which matters on a battery robot: that's about 10 mAh per day just for measuring. Larger resistors draw less, but see the next section.

## 2. Loading: the divider is a source with an output impedance

The equation above assumes **nothing** is connected to Vout. Connect a load and it sits in parallel with R2, pulling Vout down. The clean way to think about it is Thévenin's theorem, which you've used in ME5245: the divider looks like an ideal source in series with a resistance.

$$V_{th} = V_{in} \cdot \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \qquad R_{th} = R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

Rth is the divider's **output impedance**. The load forms a second divider with it:

$$V_{out} = V_{th} \cdot \frac{R_L}{R_{th} + R_L}$$

### Worked example 3: the same divider, three loads

R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ from 5 V, so Vth = 2.5 V and Rth = 5 kΩ.

| Load RL | Vout | Error vs 2.5 V |
|---|---|---|
| none (open) | 2.500 V | 0% |
| 1 MΩ (a multimeter, roughly 10 MΩ is even better) | 2.488 V | 0.5% |
| 50 kΩ | 2.273 V | 9.1% |
| 10 kΩ | 1.667 V | 33% |

The rule of thumb: **the load should be at least 10× the divider's output impedance**, and even that costs about 9% (the error is Rth / (Rth + RL) = 1/11). For a precise reading you want 100× or more, or a buffer (an op-amp voltage follower, which has very high input impedance and very low output impedance: topic A3).

This is the same input/output impedance idea from your ME5245 lectures: a source with output impedance Zout driving a load with input impedance Zin delivers Zin / (Zout + Zin) of its voltage. You want Zout small and Zin large.

**ADC input.** The ATmega328P datasheet says its ADC is optimized for sources with an output impedance of about 10 kΩ or less (its sample-and-hold capacitor has to charge through your source impedance). The 20k/10k battery divider has Rth = 6.67 kΩ: fine. A 200k/100k divider would cut the battery drain by 10× but have Rth = 66.7 kΩ, and the readings get inaccurate. The usual fix is a 100 nF capacitor from the ADC pin to ground, which supplies the sampling charge.

**A divider is not a power supply.** Never power a sensor, servo, or motor from a divider. The load current changes, so the voltage changes. Use a regulator.

## 3. Pull-up and pull-down resistors

A digital input pin has a very high input impedance (the ATmega328P's input leakage is at most about 1 µA). Leave it unconnected and it **floats**: it picks up noise and reads random 0s and 1s. You've probably seen this: a button input that "presses itself" when you touch the wire.

A **pull-up** resistor ties the input weakly to VCC so it reads 1 by default; a switch to ground overrides it and reads 0. A **pull-down** ties it weakly to ground, and a switch to VCC overrides it.

```
pull-up (active low)               pull-down (active high)
 VCC                                VCC
  |                                  |
 [R] 10k                            [switch]
  |                                  |
  +---- input pin                    +---- input pin
  |                                  |
 [switch]                           [R] 10k
  |                                  |
 GND                                GND
```

The Uno has built-in pull-ups (about 20 to 50 kΩ): `pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP);`. Then a button from pin 2 to GND reads `LOW` when pressed, `HIGH` when released. That "pressed = LOW" inversion catches every beginner once.

Current when the switch is closed: 5 V / 10 kΩ = 0.5 mA. With 4.7 kΩ, 5 / 4.7k = 1.06 mA. Smaller resistors resist noise better but waste more current; 4.7k to 10k is the usual range for buttons.

A PLC analogy: a pull-up is like a normally-closed, fail-safe input. The resistor sets the default state, and the device has to actively do something to change it.

## 4. Open-drain outputs, and why I2C needs pull-ups

A normal (push-pull) output has two transistors: one drives the pin high, one drives it low. An **open-drain** output (open-collector, for a BJT) has only the bottom transistor. It can pull the line **low**, or let go. It can never drive the line high.

I2C uses open-drain for both SDA (data) and SCL (clock), on purpose:

- **Several devices share two wires.** If two push-pull outputs disagreed (one high, one low), they would short VCC to GND through two transistors. With open-drain, the worst case is two devices both pulling low: harmless.
- **Wired-AND.** The line is high only if *every* device lets go. Any device can hold it low. That's how a slave stretches the clock (holds SCL low until it's ready), and how ACK works (the receiver pulls SDA low during the 9th clock).
- **Mixed voltages.** The high level is set by where the pull-up goes, not by each chip's supply.

So the line needs a **pull-up resistor** to give it a high level. No pull-ups: the line can never go high, and an I2C scan finds nothing (or the bus hangs). This is the first thing to check when an I2C device doesn't respond.

## 5. Choosing an I2C pull-up value

There is a window. Too big and the bus is too slow; too small and devices can't pull it low.

**Upper limit: rise time.** When every device lets go, the line charges through Rp into the bus capacitance Cb (wiring plus every pin, typically tens to a few hundred pF). The I2C spec measures rise time from 30% to 70% of VCC. For an RC charge:

$$t_r = \left(\ln\frac{0.7}{0.3}\right) R_p C_b \approx 0.8473 \cdot R_p C_b$$

The spec limits it to 1000 ns in standard mode (100 kHz, the Arduino `Wire` default) and 300 ns in fast mode (400 kHz). So

$$R_{p,max} = \frac{t_r}{0.8473 \cdot C_b}$$

Worked: Rp = 4.7 kΩ, Cb = 100 pF gives tr = 0.8473 × 4.7k × 100p = 398 ns. That's fine for 100 kHz (under 1000 ns) but too slow for 400 kHz (over 300 ns). For fast mode with Cb = 200 pF, Rp,max = 300 ns / (0.8473 × 200 pF) = 1.77 kΩ.

**Lower limit: sink current.** When a device pulls low, it sinks the pull-up current, and the spec only guarantees it can sink 3 mA while holding the line at or below 0.4 V:

$$R_{p,min} = \frac{V_{CC} - V_{OL}}{I_{OL}} = \frac{5 - 0.4}{3 \text{ mA}} = 1.53 \text{ k}\Omega \quad (\text{or } 967 \text{ } \Omega \text{ at } 3.3 \text{ V})$$

Below that, the low level isn't low enough to be read as a 0.

**In practice**: 4.7 kΩ for short wires at 100 kHz, 2.2 kΩ for 400 kHz or longer wires. And remember that pull-ups on several modules **add in parallel**: three modules each with 4.7k on board give 1.57 kΩ total, which is near the 5 V minimum.

## 6. The case you'll actually wire: a GY-521 on a 5 V Uno

The GY-521 breakout carries the MPU-6050 (a 3.3 V chip), a 3.3 V regulator, and pull-up resistors on SDA and SCL. On most boards they're 4.7 kΩ (marked `472`; some use 2.2 kΩ, `222`). Read the code on yours. Those pull-ups go to the board's **3.3 V**, not to the Uno's 5 V. So when the bus is idle, SDA and SCL sit at about 3.3 V. Is that a valid "1" for a 5 V ATmega328P?

From the ATmega328P general I/O characteristics, V_IH (the minimum voltage guaranteed to read as high) is 0.6 × VCC:

$$V_{IH,min} = 0.6 \times 5 \text{ V} = 3.0 \text{ V}$$

3.3 V is above 3.0 V, so it reads high, but the **noise margin** is only 0.3 V. That's why it works on the bench, and why it can get flaky with long wires, motor noise, or a sagging 3.3 V rail. Two more details that a careful engineer checks:

- The datasheet's **two-wire interface** table may list a stricter V_IH for the SDA/SCL pins in TWI mode (look for 0.7 × VCC = 3.5 V in your copy). If so, 3.3 V is technically below spec, and the setup works only because the real threshold is usually lower than the guaranteed one. That's exactly the kind of "works, but not by design" that a test engineer flags.
- `Wire.begin()` on the Uno enables the chip's **internal** pull-ups (20 to 50 kΩ) to 5 V. Together with the board's 4.7 kΩ to 3.3 V they form a divider, and the idle line sits a bit above 3.3 V (about 3.5 V with a 35 kΩ internal pull-up), with a few tens of µA flowing back into the 3.3 V rail. That's usually harmless, but check the MPU-6050's absolute maximum rating for its I/O pins before you trust it in a product.

The robust fixes: a bidirectional I2C level shifter (the common BSS138 MOSFET boards), or a 3.3 V microcontroller. For Sprint 1 the direct connection is standard practice and fine, and now you can explain *why* it works and what its weak point is. That explanation is an interview answer.

## 7. Level shifting a one-way signal: HC-SR04 echo into a 3.3 V device

The HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor's ECHO pin outputs 5 V pulses. Into the 5 V Uno that's fine. Into a **Raspberry Pi** (WALL-E's brain), whose GPIO is 3.3 V and **not 5 V tolerant**, 5 V can damage the pin.

For a one-way, low-speed signal, a divider is the simplest level shifter. 1 kΩ on top (from ECHO) and 2 kΩ on the bottom (to GND), with the Pi pin at the junction:

$$V_{out} = 5 \cdot \frac{2}{1 + 2} = 3.33 \text{ V}$$

Check the speed: the divider's output impedance is 1k ∥ 2k = 667 Ω, and with about 10 pF of pin capacitance the time constant is about 7 ns. Echo pulses are tens of microseconds to milliseconds long, so the divider doesn't distort them.

Common mistakes here: swapping the resistors (2k on top gives 1.67 V, which may not read as high), and using a divider on a **bidirectional** line like I2C (a divider only works in one direction, and it would fight the open-drain pull-downs). The TRIG input goes the other way (Pi to sensor); many HC-SR04 modules accept a 3.3 V trigger, but check yours.

## 8. Reading a datasheet table

Interviewers love "read this table" questions. Here's an abridged version of the ATmega328P's DC characteristics, for VCC = 5 V (check the full table in the datasheet for every condition):

| Parameter | Symbol | Condition | Min | Max |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Input low voltage | V_IL | general I/O pins | −0.5 V | 0.3 × VCC = 1.5 V |
| Input high voltage | V_IH | general I/O pins | 0.6 × VCC = 3.0 V | VCC + 0.5 V |
| Output low voltage | V_OL | sinking 20 mA | | 0.9 V |
| Output high voltage | V_OH | sourcing 20 mA | 4.2 V | |
| I/O pull-up resistor | R_PU | | 20 kΩ | 50 kΩ |

How to read it:

- **Min and max are guarantees across every part, temperature, and supply in the stated range.** Typical values (when listed) are what you'll usually see, not what you can design to.
- **Conditions matter.** V_OL is 0.9 V max *when sinking 20 mA*. Sink less and it's lower.
- **The gap between V_IL max and V_IH min (1.5 V to 3.0 V) is undefined.** A signal sitting at 2.2 V might read as 0 or 1, and might change with temperature. Design to stay out of it.
- **Noise margin** compares a driver with a receiver: NM_high = V_OH,min(driver) − V_IH,min(receiver) and NM_low = V_IL,max(receiver) − V_OL,max(driver). For the GY-521's open-drain SDA (high = 3.3 V pull-up, low ≤ 0.4 V) into the Uno: NM_high = 3.3 − 3.0 = 0.3 V and NM_low = 1.5 − 0.4 = 1.1 V. The high side is the weak one.

## Common mistakes

- Putting R1 instead of R2 in the numerator of the divider equation.
- Ignoring loading: computing a divider output, then connecting a load comparable to Rth.
- Powering something from a divider.
- Leaving an input floating ("it worked yesterday").
- Forgetting that `INPUT_PULLUP` makes a pressed button read `LOW`.
- No I2C pull-ups, or too many in parallel from several breakout boards.
- Using a resistor divider on a bidirectional line (I2C).
- Designing to "typical" instead of min/max datasheet values.
