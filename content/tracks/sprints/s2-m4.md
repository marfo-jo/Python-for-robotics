## Goal, and why it matters

Close the loop the way an engineer does: **model first, then design, then predict, then measure, then explain the difference.** Anyone can turn knobs until a motor behaves. Very few co-op candidates can say "I designed the gains from an identified model, predicted a 0.10 s rise time, measured 0.11 s, and here's why it's slower."

PID questions come up in almost every controls and robotics interview. Your answer will include integrator windup, actuator saturation, sample-rate limits, and sensor quantization, because you'll have hit all four.

## Step 1: the design, on paper

Your plant, from 2.3 (u is the duty fraction, ω in rad/s):

$$G(s) = \frac{K}{\tau s + 1}$$

A PI controller acts on the error e = ω_ref - ω:

$$C(s) = K_p + \frac{K_i}{s} = K_p \frac{T_i s + 1}{T_i s} \qquad T_i = \frac{K_p}{K_i}$$

**Why PI, and not P or PID?**

- **P alone** leaves a steady-state error: to hold a speed, the motor needs a nonzero duty, which a P controller only produces from a nonzero error. (Work out the size of that error for your K.)
- **The integral term** builds up the duty needed to hold the speed while the error goes to zero.
- **D** helps when there's overshoot to damp. A first-order plant under PI control doesn't need it, and differentiating a quantized encoder speed mostly amplifies noise.

**The pole-zero cancellation design.** Choose T_i = τ, so the controller's zero cancels the plant's pole. Then the loop gain C(s)·G(s) becomes a pure integrator, and the closed loop from ω_ref to ω is **first order** again, with a time constant τ_cl that you choose with Kp. Do this algebra yourself:

1. Write C(s)·G(s) with T_i = τ and simplify.
2. Find the closed-loop transfer function C·G / (1 + C·G) and put it in the form 1 / (τ_cl s + 1).
3. Solve for Kp in terms of τ, K, and τ_cl; then Ki from T_i = τ.

Predicted response to a setpoint step: no overshoot, zero steady-state error, and a 10 to 90% **rise time of 2.2 τ_cl** (that number comes from the first-order step response: work it out from ln(0.9) and ln(0.1)).

**Choosing τ_cl: it's a trade-off, not a free choice.**

- **Faster** (smaller τ_cl) needs bigger Kp, which means the duty **saturates** at ±100% on large steps (the plant can't go faster than full voltage), and amplifies encoder **quantization noise** into PWM jitter.
- The loop runs at **10 ms**, and the speed measurement adds delay. A rule of thumb: keep τ_cl at least 5 to 10 times the total loop delay.
- A good first choice: τ_cl between τ/2 and τ/3. State what you picked and why.

## Step 2: the discrete controller on the Uno

Every 10 ms tick (Ts = 0.01 s):

1. **Measure the speed** from the encoder count change. Over one tick the quantization is coarse (2.2 did the math: about 0.42 rad/s per count at CPR ≈ 1500). Differencing over a short window of ticks smooths it at the cost of delay. Choose, and write down the delay you added.
2. **Error** e = setpoint - measured speed.
3. **Integrate**: the integral state accumulates Ki · e · Ts.
4. **Output** u = Kp · e + integral state, as a duty fraction.
5. **Saturate**: clamp u to [-1, 1], then PWM = u × 255.
6. **Anti-windup.** When u is saturated, the integral keeps growing even though the motor can't respond. When the error finally reverses, the oversized integral must unwind first, causing a large overshoot. That's **integrator windup**. The simplest fix, *conditional integration*: don't integrate on a tick when the output is saturated **and** the error would push it further into saturation. (Another: clamp the integral state itself to [-1, 1].)

Everything else from 2.1 stays: the command buffer, the watchdog (on timeout: setpoint 0, PWM 0, and **reset the integral**), and the stream.

**Commands to add** (suggested):

| Command | Effect |
|---|---|
| `SPD <rad/s>` | closed-loop mode with this setpoint (a decimal number; the AVR's `atof` handles it) |
| `GAINS <kp> <ki>` | set the gains without re-uploading; reply with a `#` line echoing them |
| `PWM <n>` | back to open-loop mode, as in 2.1 |
| `STOP` | setpoint 0, PWM 0, integral reset |

**Stream:** append the setpoint to the 2.1 line: `t_ms,pwm,count,shunt_raw,bus_raw,temp_c,setpoint`. Your `rig.py` parser should accept both formats (that's a good reason to read the header line instead of assuming the columns).

**Integer or float?** The ATmega328P has no floating-point hardware, but a handful of float operations per 10 ms tick takes well under a millisecond. Floats are fine here; measure the loop time with `micros()` to prove it.

## Step 3: predict in Python before you run it

Write `host/pi_design.py` that computes Kp and Ki from your K, τ, and chosen τ_cl, then **simulates the closed loop the way the Uno runs it**: a loop at Ts = 10 ms where each tick computes the PI output from the current (simulated) speed, applies saturation and your anti-windup rule, holds that duty for one tick, and advances the plant one tick with the exact update from 2.3 (`simulate_first_order` over two time points, or the same formula inline).

Compare three predictions on one plot for a large step: the ideal first-order response (2.2 τ_cl rise time), the simulation with saturation and anti-windup, and the simulation with saturation but **without** anti-windup. That plot, before any hardware run, shows you what to expect.

Optional realism: add the speed-measurement window and quantization (round the simulated count) to the simulation and see how much of the measured behavior it explains.

## Step 4: measure and compare

Write a new plan for your 2.2 runner with setpoint steps (for example 0 → 30% and 0 → 60% of your maximum no-load speed, then back to 0, each repeated 3 times), and run it. Then compute, from both the prediction and the measurement:

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Rise time | time from 10% to 90% of the step |
| Overshoot | (peak - final) / step size, in % |
| Settling time | time until the speed stays within ±2% of the final value |
| Steady-state error | setpoint minus the mean speed over the last second |

Then **explain the differences**. The usual suspects: deadband and dry friction (the model has none), saturation, the speed-estimation delay, quantization noise, temperature (K drifts as the motor warms), and supply sag (check the bus voltage column).

## Common beginner mistakes

- Designing the gains in rpm and running them on rad/s (or duty in 0 to 255 vs 0 to 1). Pick units once, write them next to every gain.
- Forgetting Ts in the integral, so the gain depends on the loop rate.
- No saturation in the simulation, so the prediction is impossibly fast.
- Not resetting the integral on `STOP` or on a watchdog stop: the next start jumps.
- Tuning by hand until it looks good, then calling it "model-based." Run the designed gains first and report them, even if you tweak afterwards (and report the tweak too).
- Differencing the speed over one tick and wondering why the PWM chatters.

<details><summary>Hint 2 (approach)</summary>

With T_i = τ, the loop gain reduces to Kp·K / (τ s), the closed loop is 1 / ((τ / (Kp·K)) s + 1), so τ_cl = τ / (Kp·K). Solve that for Kp, and Ki = Kp / τ. For the discrete simulation: keep the integral state and the plant speed as two variables, loop over ticks, and update them in the same order the firmware does.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 3 (pseudocode): one controller tick (the same in C and in your simulation)</summary>

```
speed = measured speed (count change over your window, converted to rad/s)
e = setpoint - speed
u_unsat = Kp * e + integ
u = clamp(u_unsat, -1, 1)
saturated = (u != u_unsat)
if not (saturated and e has the same sign as u_unsat):
    integ = integ + Ki * e * Ts
    (optionally clamp integ to [-1, 1])
apply duty u (sign -> direction pins, |u| * 255 -> PWM)

on STOP or watchdog: setpoint = 0; integ = 0; apply duty 0
```

</details>

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Speed oscillates around the setpoint | Kp too large for the loop delay, or the speed window adds more delay than you thought | Increase τ_cl; shorten the window; check the loop rate. |
| Steady-state error never goes to zero | Integral not running (gain 0, or always "saturated"), or Ts missing | Print the integral state. |
| Huge overshoot after big steps only | Integrator windup | Anti-windup; compare on and off. |
| PWM chatters audibly at steady state | Quantized speed times a big Kp | Longer speed window or a smaller Kp. |
| Small setpoints never get there, or limit-cycle | Deadband: the integral has to build past the static friction | Normal; quantify it. Feedforward of the deadband duty is a stretch goal. |
| Motor runs away in one direction | Feedback sign wrong (encoder sign vs PWM sign) | Positive PWM must give positive measured speed. Check in open loop first. |
| Response is slower than predicted for big steps | Saturation: the plant is at full duty | Expected; your saturated simulation should predict it. |
| Loop slows down or the watchdog fires | A blocking DS18B20 read or long prints inside the tick | Measure the tick with `micros()`; keep the non-blocking temperature service. |
