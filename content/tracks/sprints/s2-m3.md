## Step 1: the equations of motion

A brushed DC motor is two coupled systems.

**Electrical** (the armature winding: resistance R, inductance L, back-EMF proportional to speed ω):

$$V = R i + L \frac{di}{dt} + K_e \omega$$

**Mechanical** (rotor and gearbox inertia J, viscous friction b, torque proportional to current, load torque):

$$J \frac{d\omega}{dt} = K_t i - b \omega - \tau_L$$

In SI units, the torque constant K_t (N·m/A) and the back-EMF constant K_e (V·s/rad) are the same number: it's the same magnetic coupling seen from two sides (energy conservation).

**The reduction to first order.** The electrical time constant τ_e = L / R is typically well under a millisecond for a small motor like this; the mechanical response takes tens to hundreds of milliseconds. When one time constant is that much faster, you can treat the fast part as instantaneous: set L di/dt ≈ 0, solve the electrical equation for i, and substitute into the mechanical one (with no external load). Do that algebra yourself and you'll get

$$\tau_m \frac{d\omega}{dt} + \omega = K V$$

and expressions for the time constant τ_m and gain K in terms of J, R, b, K_t, K_e. Put the derivation in your README. Then notice what it predicts and check it in your data:

- The step response is a **first-order exponential**: no overshoot, reaching 63% of its final value at t = τ.
- The **current spike** at the step: at t = 0, ω = 0, so no back-EMF, and the current is limited only by R. As the motor speeds up, back-EMF grows and the current falls. You saw this in 2.2.

**What the model leaves out** (and what you'll see in the residuals): Coulomb (dry) friction, which makes the **deadband**; the L298N's voltage drop, which isn't a constant fraction of the duty; gearbox backlash; and the fact that commanding PWM 0 makes the L298N **coast** (an open circuit), so the coast-down is governed by friction alone, not by the same τ. That's why you fit only the **rising** part of each step.

**The input.** Use u = duty fraction (`pwm / 255`, from -1 to 1), so K is in rad/s per unit duty. (A refinement for later: u = duty × measured bus voltage, so supply sag is accounted for.)

## Step 2: identifying K and τ

For a step of size u at time t₀ from rest, the model's response is

$$\omega(t) = K u \left(1 - e^{-(t - t_0)/\tau}\right) \qquad t \ge t_0$$

Two unknowns. The fitting method you'll implement:

1. **Steady state first.** Average the speed over the last part of the step (the spec: the last 10% of the samples after t₀) to get ω_ss. Then K = ω_ss / u. Averaging matters: the peak of a noisy signal overestimates the steady state.
2. **Linearize the rise.** Rearranging the response,

$$\ln\left(1 - \frac{\omega}{\omega_{ss}}\right) = -\frac{t - t_0}{\tau}$$

   so the left side plotted against (t - t₀) is a **straight line through the origin with slope -1/τ**. Fit that line by least squares and take τ from the slope.
3. **Only use the rise.** Use samples where ω/ω_ss is between 0.05 and 0.9. Near 0 the value is dominated by quantization; near 1 the log of a small, noisy number explodes (and for ω ≥ ω_ss it's the log of zero or a negative number). Points from the flat tail, where noise occasionally dips below 0.9, can wreck the fit; one of the site's tests checks for exactly that, so think about it.
4. **Measure time from the step.** Use (t - t₀), not the time since the log started. With samples before the step in the log, using the wrong origin silently ruins τ.

Least squares on a linearized model is the simplest honest method. A direct nonlinear fit of the exponential is better with heavy noise; you'll learn the tools in Module 3 and can come back to compare.

## Step 3: simulating the model

To compare against measurement, simulate the model with the **measured** input u(t) at the **measured** timestamps (which aren't perfectly evenly spaced).

Between two samples the input is constant (the firmware holds the PWM until the next command): a **zero-order hold**. A first-order system with a constant input has an exact solution, so the update from sample k to k+1 is exact:

$$\omega_{k+1} = a \omega_k + (1 - a) K u_k \qquad a = e^{-\Delta t_k / \tau}$$

with Δt_k = t_{k+1} - t_k. Derive it yourself from the step response above: over one interval, ω decays exponentially from ω_k toward K u_k.

**Why not forward Euler** (ω_{k+1} = ω_k + (Δt/τ)(K u_k - ω_k))? It's an approximation that's only accurate when Δt is much smaller than τ, and it goes **unstable** when Δt > 2τ: the simulated speed oscillates and grows. The exact update is stable for any Δt and costs the same. One of the site's tests uses Δt > 2τ on purpose.

## Step 4: measuring the match

The **coefficient of determination**:

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y - \hat y)^2}{\sum (y - \bar y)^2}$$

1 means a perfect match; 0 means "no better than predicting the mean." Compute it on data **not used for the fit** (held-out repeats). R² on the fitted data flatters the model; R² on held-out data is the honest number, and it's the one that goes in your report and resume bullet.

## `motor_model.py`, the Python spec

numpy only (scipy is not installed and not needed). Names and signatures must match.

### `fit_first_order(t, omega, u, t0=None) -> tuple[float, float]`

Return `(K, tau)` from one step test.

- `t`: 1-D array of times in seconds, **strictly increasing**. `omega`: speeds, same shape. `u`: the step size (a nonzero number; may be negative). `t0`: time of the step; default `t[0]`.
- Use only samples with `t >= t0`, with time measured from `t0`.
- ω_ss = the mean of the **last 10%** of those samples (at least one sample). K = ω_ss / u.
- τ from a least-squares fit of the linearized rise (Step 2), using samples with 0.05 < ω/ω_ss < 0.9. A line through the origin is the model; a line with an intercept is also acceptable.
- Raise `ValueError` if: the lengths differ; `t` isn't strictly increasing; `u == 0`; there are fewer than 10 samples after `t0`; the motor didn't really move, meaning |ω_ss| is less than 5 times the standard deviation of the samples it was averaged from (a duty inside the deadband); or fewer than 3 samples fall in the rise window.

### `simulate_first_order(t, u, K, tau, omega0=0.0) -> numpy array`

Simulate the model with the exact zero-order-hold update (Step 3).

- `t`: strictly increasing times (uneven spacing allowed). `u`: a number, or an array the same length as `t`, where `u[k]` is held from `t[k]` to `t[k+1]`.
- Returns an array the same length as `t` with `result[0] = omega0`.
- Raise `ValueError` if `tau <= 0`, `t` isn't strictly increasing, or an array `u` has the wrong length.

### `r_squared(measured, predicted) -> float`

As in Step 4. Raise `ValueError` if the shapes differ, there are fewer than 2 points, or `measured` is constant.

## Step 5: your data

Write `host/compare_model.py` that, for one run:

1. Loads the log, computes speed (differencing over a few samples, as discussed in 2.2), and splits it into steps using `step_id` and `phase`.
2. Fits K and τ to the rising part of each step (t₀ = the first sample of the "step" phase).
3. For each PWM level: fits on repeat 1, simulates repeats 2 and 3 with their own `u(t)` and timestamps, and computes R².
4. Produces the plots and a table (level, direction, K, τ, R² held-out).

Expect τ to be roughly the same at every level (it's a property of the motor), and K to vary near the deadband (the model has no dry friction). If τ varies a lot, look for a mechanical cause.

## Common beginner mistakes

- Fitting the whole step including the rest period or the coast-down.
- Time measured from the start of the log instead of from the step.
- K from the peak speed.
- Taking the log of values ≥ 1 (NaN warnings) because the rise window wasn't restricted.
- Forward Euler with Δt comparable to τ.
- Reporting R² on the data you fitted.
- Mixing rpm and rad/s, or counts and radians. Convert once, in `rig.py`.

<details><summary>Hint 2 (approach)</summary>

`fit_first_order`: validate; select the samples at or after t₀ with a boolean mask; shift time; average the last tenth for ω_ss; check it against 5 standard deviations; compute the ratio; mask the rise window; take the log; fit the slope (for a line through the origin, the least-squares slope is the sum of x·y divided by the sum of x²; `np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` gives a line with an intercept); τ = -1 / slope.

`simulate_first_order`: validate; turn a scalar `u` into an array of the right length; allocate the output; loop from k = 0 to N-2 computing `a` from that interval's Δt.

</details>

<details><summary>Hint 3 (pseudocode)</summary>

```
fit_first_order(t, omega, u, t0):
    validate lengths, strictly increasing t, u != 0
    t0 = t[0] if not given
    keep = t >= t0; tt = t[keep] - t0; w = omega[keep]
    if fewer than 10 samples: raise ValueError
    tail = last max(1, len(w) // 10) samples of w
    w_ss = mean(tail); if |w_ss| < 5 * std(tail): raise ValueError
    K = w_ss / u
    r = w / w_ss
    rise = 0.05 < r < 0.9; if fewer than 3: raise ValueError
    y = ln(1 - r[rise]); x = tt[rise]
    slope = least-squares slope of y against x
    return K, -1 / slope

simulate_first_order(t, u, K, tau, omega0):
    validate tau > 0, t strictly increasing, u length
    w[0] = omega0
    for k from 0 to N - 2:
        a = exp(-(t[k+1] - t[k]) / tau)
        w[k+1] = a * w[k] + (1 - a) * K * u[k]
    return w
```

</details>

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| `RuntimeWarning: invalid value encountered in log` | ω/ω_ss ≥ 1 in the fit window | Restrict to 0.05 to 0.9. |
| τ comes out much too large | Time origin wrong, or flat-tail points in the fit | Measure from t₀; check the window. |
| τ is negative | Sign problem: u and ω have opposite signs, or the slope sign is flipped | Check that negative PWM gives negative speed in `rig.py`. |
| K differs between directions | Real: the motor and gearbox aren't perfectly symmetric | Report both. |
| R² is great on fitted data, poor on held-out data | Over-trusting one noisy repeat, or the motor warmed up between repeats | Check the temperature column; fit on a mean of repeats. |
| Simulation oscillates or explodes | Forward Euler with a big Δt | Use the exact update. |
| Simulation lags the measurement by one sample | Using `u[k+1]` instead of `u[k]` for the interval starting at `t[k]` | The input in effect over [t_k, t_k+1) is `u[k]`. |
| Simulated speed never moves for the small steps | Those levels are inside the deadband | Exclude them from the fit and document the deadband. |
