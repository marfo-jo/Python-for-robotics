## Safety

An endurance test is the longest your rig will ever run, and heat is the main risk.

- **Supervise it.** Stay in the room. Don't run it overnight or while you're out.
- **Temperature limit**: abort above 60 °C on the motor can (lower it if your motor's listing gives a lower rating). Also feel nothing: use the DS18B20 log, not your fingers.
- **Current limit with a time condition** (as in 2.2), and a **low bus voltage** limit (a sagging supply or a blown fuse).
- **Cool-down**: if a run aborts on temperature, let the motor cool to room temperature before the next attempt.
- The L298N heatsink gets hot during long runs; make sure it has air around it and nothing flammable on it.

## Step 1: define the cycle and the criteria

A **load cycle** is one repeatable unit of work. A simple one for a speed rig:

| Phase | Setpoint | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| ramp up | step to the working speed (for example 60% of max) | 3 s |
| hold | working speed | 4 s |
| stop | 0 | 3 s |

That's 10 s per cycle, so 360 cycles per hour. A heavier cycle (higher speed, shorter rest) heats the motor faster; choose and justify.

**Per-cycle metrics** (one summary row per cycle):

| Metric | How to compute it | Why it might drift |
|---|---|---|
| duty needed to hold the speed | mean PWM over the last 2 s of "hold" | winding resistance rises with temperature (copper: about +0.39% per °C), so the controller needs more voltage for the same speed |
| mean current during hold | mean INA219 current | friction and temperature effects |
| steady-state speed error | setpoint minus the mean speed over the hold | should stay near 0 if the integral works; drift here is a controller problem |
| rise time | 10 to 90% of each step | the plant's K changes with temperature |
| motor-can temperature | DS18B20 at the end of the cycle | the thermal time constant of the motor |
| bus voltage | mean INA219 bus voltage | supply sag; proves the input stayed constant |

**Write the requirements first**, with numbers, before you run the test. Deciding limits after you see the data isn't a test, it's a story. (In regulated industries this is the difference between a validation and a demo.)

## Step 2: the runner

Reuse your 2.2 structure: a plan file (cycle definition, N, limits), heartbeat, safety limits, `finally:` stop, metadata JSON. New pieces:

- A per-cycle summary written as each cycle finishes, to a second CSV (so a crash at cycle 250 still leaves 249 summary rows).
- A status line every 10 cycles: cycle number, temperature, duty, error. You'll be watching it.
- Flush both files about once a second (see [writing a CSV log](#/t/sprints/bg-csv)).

## Step 3: trend and judge

Plot each per-cycle metric against cycle number (one subplot each, sharing the x-axis), and the hold duty and current against **temperature** (a scatter plot). Fit a straight line to each trend with `np.polyfit`, and report the slope as "change per 100 cycles" and "change per °C."

Things to look for, and to explain:

- The **warm-up** curve in temperature: it rises fast, then levels off as heat loss balances heat input. A first-order thermal response, the same mathematics as 2.3.
- Hold duty rising with temperature while the speed error stays near 0: the controller is compensating for drift. That's the integral term doing its job, and it's a nice result.
- Anything that doesn't match the physics: a sudden step (a loose coupler?), a periodic pattern (the supply? the room?), a slow change that doesn't follow temperature (wear?).

Then apply your pass/fail criteria, one by one, and state the verdict and the margin ("max speed error 0.8%, limit 2%: PASS").

## Step 4: the one-page report

One page, PDF, written for an engineering manager who has two minutes. Template:

1. **Title, date, author, rig and firmware version** (git tag).
2. **Objective**: one sentence. ("Determine whether the motor rig's speed regulation and electrical behavior drift over 360 load cycles, against the criteria below.")
3. **Setup**: a small photo or wiring diagram, the parts, the supply setting, the room temperature.
4. **Method**: the cycle definition, N, the safety limits, and the requirements with numbers.
5. **Results**: a small table: identified model (K, τ, R² held-out from 2.3), PI predicted vs measured rise time (2.4), and the endurance metrics (start, end, drift per 100 cycles). One or two plots, not more.
6. **Verdict**: PASS or FAIL per criterion, with margins.
7. **Issues and root causes**: anything that went wrong during the campaign and what you did about it (an Uno reset from motor noise, a watchdog trip, a fuse). Honest problems with root causes read better than a perfect report.
8. **Next steps**: one or two lines.

Write it in Markdown or Word and export to PDF. Read it out loud once: if a sentence doesn't carry a fact, cut it.

## Common beginner mistakes

- Setting the pass/fail limits after looking at the data.
- Starting the test with a warm motor, so the warm-up isn't in the data.
- Trending only temperature. The interesting result is what changes **with** temperature.
- Plotting 360 cycles of raw 100 Hz data on one chart. Summarize per cycle first.
- A two-page "one-page report."
- Leaving it running unattended.

<details><summary>Hint 3 (pseudocode)</summary>

```
endurance:
    plan = load the plan; reason = "completed"
    open link, sample log, summary log (with-blocks)
    try:
        for cycle in 1..N:
            samples = []
            for each phase in the cycle:
                reason_now = run_phase(..., collect into samples)
                if reason_now: reason = reason_now; stop everything
            metrics = summarize(samples)          (duty, current, error, rise time, temp, bus V)
            write the summary row; every 10 cycles print a status line
    finally:
        STOP; flush; write metadata with reason and cycles completed

analysis:
    load the summary CSV
    for each metric: plot against cycle; fit a line; report slope per 100 cycles
    scatter duty and current against temperature; fit; report slope per degree C
    for each requirement: compute the worst case; compare with the limit; print PASS/FAIL and margin
```

</details>

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Aborts on temperature before the end | The cycle is too heavy for the motor | Lighter cycle (lower speed, longer rest): a valid finding to report. |
| The Uno resets after many cycles | Noise, or a connection loosening with vibration | Check the screw terminals and the star ground; look for a second header in the raw log. |
| A sudden step in the hold duty | A mechanical change (coupler slipping) or the supply changed | Check the bus voltage column; inspect the mechanics. |
| Hold duty saturates near the end | The motor is too hot for the working speed | Report it: that's an operating limit you found. |
| Python falls behind after an hour | Memory growing (keeping every sample) or printing too much | Keep only the current cycle's samples in memory. |
| Laptop goes to sleep mid-test | Power settings | Turn sleep off for the test (see 1.7). |
